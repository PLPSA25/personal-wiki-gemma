# Economics Lectures (MBA 201A, Fall A)

Personal study summary of the nine microeconomics lectures of MBA 201A (Fall A 2026). Written in my own words from the
lecture slides; the slides themselves are not included. Each lecture is one section. Numbers and examples are the ones
used in class; where a slide was only a chart, this summary describes what the chart was used to show.

## Lecture 1: Competitive Markets

**Why microeconomics.** Smart people fall into predictable traps: "we already put $2 billion in, we can't walk away" (sunk
cost), "the division is profitable on the income statement" (accounting profit is not economic profit), "the warehouse is
ours, so it is free" (owned resources still have an opportunity cost), and "we charge cost plus a fair markup" (price on
value, not cost). They also hold predictable bad beliefs: a supplier doubling prices is "gouging" (prices move when scarcity
moves), "costs went up so we pass it along" (the market decides pass-through), "we are the only game in town" (demand still
constrains a monopolist), and "there is plenty of business for everyone" (one hungry rival undercutting erodes margins to
cost). The course promises to cut through this confusion.

**Force-Trap-Edge.** Every big idea in the course has three parts: a Force (how the world works), a Trap (the expensive
mistake made by ignoring it) and an Edge (the value you can create or capture by using it). Gravity is the worked example.

**Market experiment.** The class traded in a double auction modeled on real commodity exchanges. Buyers privately know their
value for each unit, sellers privately know their cost for each unit, and each side keeps the difference between the price
and their value or cost. A buyer's value falls with each extra unit (diminishing marginal utility, like the fourth espresso
of the day) and a seller's cost rises with each extra unit (rising marginal cost, like a driller's easiest well pumping
first). Sorting all buyer units by value gives market demand; sorting all seller units by cost gives market supply. In the
two-buyer example, at a price of $7 each buyer wants two units and demand is 4 units; the sellers likewise supply 4 units.

**Equilibrium and the invisible hand.** Below equilibrium there is excess demand and rivals bid the price up; above it there
is excess supply and rivals undercut. Where both vanish the market is at rest. In the class market of ten traders the
"equilibrium tunnel" was a price between $6.50 and $7.50 with a quantity of 10, and total gains from trade were $55 ($27.50
to buyers, $27.50 to sellers, if every trade struck at $7). The outcome is efficient in three ways: the right quantity (the
11th unit is worth $6.50 but costs $7.50, so trading it would destroy $1), the right producers (the ten cheapest units get
made) and the right consumers (the ten highest-value units get bought). Nobody controls the price and nobody sees the whole
picture; nobody was in charge in the room either.

**Trap: fighting the equilibrium price.** Pricing below equilibrium creates queues, scalpers and lost profit (concert
tickets, sneaker drops, hurricane anti-gouging caps that leave stations with no gas). Pricing above it means you do not sell
(a house listed above the tunnel).

**Comparative statics.** When fundamentals change, price and quantity move predictably. Values rising shifts demand up and
raises both price and quantity. Costs rising shifts supply up and raises price but lowers quantity. Both rising together
raises price while quantity may not move; in class, values and costs all rose about $4 and the tunnel jumped by about $4 with
the same number of trades. Price alone cannot show which curve moved; price and quantity together can.

**Caveats.** "Markets are efficient" has fine print: externalities, market power and hidden information. Supply-and-demand
reasoning is still a useful guide to the direction of prices and quantities.

**Course logistics.** Grading: participation 30%, quizzes 10%, midterm 15%, final exam 20%, group memo 15%, Examples in the
Wild 10%. The next lecture covers economic cost.

## Lecture 2: The Concept of Economic Cost

**Economic versus accounting cost.** An accountant measures all expenditures and allocates them across activities, which is
useful for understanding what has happened. Economic cost is forward looking and depends on the value of alternatives:
economic cost = expenditure (accounting cost) - sunk costs + opportunity costs.

**Opportunity cost.** The opportunity cost of an action is the value of the most highly valued alternative given up. Making
guacamole for $10 of ingredients instead of buying it for $20 only saves money if your hour of time is worth less than $10.
Opportunity cost of time is a relevant economic cost. Nothing you own is free: the Tokyo firm's basement farm costs its
expenditure plus the profit forgone from the next best use of that real estate.

**Sunk costs.** Costs that cannot be changed are irrelevant to forward-looking decisions. If you lose a $50 ticket at the
door, attending costs $50, not $100. Whether to leave a disappointing event does not depend on whether you paid $50 or $500.
Worked example: $5m already spent (sunk), $10m more to launch, $11m of revenue, and $2m of profit forgone by diverting the
$10m. Economic cost = 15 - 5 + 2 = 12, benefits minus cost = -1, so stop. Three habits avoid mistakes: ignore sunk costs,
always ask for the opportunity cost, and think like an outsider. A brewery bonus example (launching a seltzer line) flips
from "+$150k" on accounting numbers to "-$150k" once the $300k forgone warehouse lease is counted; the $400k of R&D and
$700k of idle equipment are sunk.

**Option value.** Sunk costs concern a door that has already closed; irreversibility also matters looking forward. When a
choice is hard to undo, keeping options open has value. Example: keeping a money-losing plant such as Diablo Canyon open can
be right, because the loss buys the right to decide again with more information, while closing is a one-way door.

**Inventory.** An item's economic cost is its replacement cost if you will need another unit, or its resale value if not;
the price you paid is sunk. A bike shop that bought at $750 while the price rose to $1,000 should treat $1,000 as its cost.
The same logic applies to businesses stocking up ahead of tariffs: if they will restock at the tariffed price, they should
raise prices now.

**Cost disease (Baumol).** When productivity in a sector does not keep pace with rising wages elsewhere, its costs climb.
Playing a Beethoven quartet takes the same 2.66 hours as in 1826, but the opportunity cost of that labor is about 23 times
higher (real wages from $1.14 to $26.44). Higher education, health care, the performing arts and personal services are
affected; fast technology growth and the ability to shift inputs protect against it.

**Sunk costs and institutions.** Kodak invented much of digital photography yet could not pivot after dominating film.
Sunk-cost thinking can be psychological (loss aversion, fear of admitting a mistake) but also institutional, since divisions
fight to justify their existence. Related idea: counter positioning, where incumbents cannot copy a new model without
damaging their existing business. Taking an outsider's view helps.

**Transfer pricing.** If Azure sells a unit externally for $100 and it costs $30 to make, an internal team that values it at
$60 should be charged the opportunity cost: $100 when capacity binds, $30 when capacity is slack. The answer is "it depends."

**Cost of capital.** Investors' next-best return is a real cost that never appears on the income statement. A firm with $5m
of accounting profit on $100m of capital with an 8% cost of capital has economic profit of $5m - $8m = -$3m: positive
accounting profit but value destroyed. In the long run (Lecture 8) competition pushes economic profit to zero.

## Lecture 3: Cost Curves and Supply

**Two firm decisions.** A price-taking firm decides how much to produce and whether to produce at all (the shutdown
decision). Vocabulary: fixed cost (paid whether or not, and regardless of how much, you produce), variable cost (changes with
quantity), total cost = fixed + variable, and marginal cost (MC), the extra cost of one more unit.

**The Golden Rule.** A price taker produces the quantity where marginal cost equals price, MC(q) = P: keep going while MC is
below price and stop when the next unit would cost more. In the Rise & Grind bakery example the price is $12 and the marginal
cost of batches 1 to 5 is $6, $8, $11, $15 and $20, so the bakery bakes 3 batches. Marginal cost is flat or falling at
first but eventually rises (crowded assembly line, running out of qualified workers, scarce inputs, harder management). Paying
higher wages does not automatically mean higher cost: Costco's wage premium buys lower turnover and more sales per employee.
Because the firm supplies where MC = P at every price, a price-taking firm's marginal-cost curve is its supply curve. Rule for
thinking like a microeconomist: make decisions on the margin.

**Shutdown decision.** Producing the golden-rule quantity must also beat producing nothing. The bakery's three batches earn
$11 above marginal cost (6 + 4 + 1) against a $40 oven lease it could avoid by staying closed, so opening loses $29 while
staying closed loses $0. Average cost is fixed cost per unit plus marginal cost, a U-shape that MC crosses at its minimum. If
the fixed cost is avoidable, the firm needs a price of at least the minimum of average cost to operate; above that floor supply
is the MC curve, below it quantity supplied is zero.

**From one firm to the market.** Market supply is the horizontal sum of firms' supply curves (add quantities at each price,
"sideways, not up"). Low-cost firms have flatter MC and earn surplus; the marginal firm just breaks even. A real example is the
Texas grid's supply stack, cheapest generators first and peakers last. In the long run, entry, capacity changes and
imitation flatten supply and compete profits away, unless a cost edge cannot be copied (Lecture 8).

**Producing and shutting down in the wild.** "We'll make it up on volume" fails when price is below marginal cost, because
each extra unit deepens the loss (MoviePass paid theaters roughly full price for a $10 monthly plan, signed over 2 million
members and burned about $21.7M a month). Sometimes other avoidable costs complicate shutdown: airlines flew near-empty
"ghost flights" in 2020 to keep airport slots, and coal and gas plants sometimes run at a small loss because restarting costs
fuel and time. Trap: the average-cost fallacy, shutting down or refusing orders whenever price is below average cost even
though price covers every cost you can still avoid.

**Shared-overhead fallacy.** Any unit priced above marginal cost adds money. Turning down a $9 order because "full cost is $11"
when marginal cost is $7 gives up $2 per unit. John Deere spread shared overhead per unit across parts, which inflated the
apparent cost of the next part and made its in-house shop lose bids. The rule of thumb: an expense is a direct cost of a part
only if making the part causes it; treat unallocable overhead as sunk for the operating decision (it bears on exit, not on the
next unit). Ice-cream example: a $1,000 per day machine, vanilla costs $0.20 a cone and bacon $0.80, both sell for $1.50, with
900 vanilla and 100 bacon cones demanded. Allocating the machine cost makes bacon look like a $30 loser, but each bacon cone
adds $0.70 above its avoidable cost, so sell them.

**Takeaways.** A price taker's supply curve is its marginal-cost curve; shutdown is a separate question about avoidable costs;
dividing fixed costs per unit corrupts the next-unit decision; market supply is the horizontal sum of firms' MC curves.

## Lecture 4: Demand and Comparative Statics

**Demand is marginal benefit.** A buyer's demand curve comes from their marginal benefit, which equals willingness to pay
(the most they would pay for one more unit). Marginal benefit falls with each extra unit (diminishing marginal utility), so in
the donut example a $1.60 price means buying the units worth more than $1.60. Market demand is the horizontal sum of buyers'
demand curves, just like market supply in Lecture 3, and it is flatter and further out than any single buyer.

**Movements versus shifts.** Demand holds everything else equal (ceteris paribus). A change in the good's own price is a
movement along the curve; any other change is a shift. Shifters of demand include prices of substitutes and complements,
income (normal goods rise with income, luxury goods rise more than proportionally, inferior goods fall), features, ecosystem
and expectations. The same distinction applies to supply: input costs, technology and weather shift it, while its own price
moves you along it. Comparative statics comes down to deciding which of these two pictures an event calls for.

**Equilibrium in competitive markets.** The lecture assumes many buyers and sellers who are price takers, a clean benchmark
that is close to commodities, financial markets and wholesale markets. Above the equilibrium price there is excess supply
that pushes price down; below it excess demand pushes price up. Uber after a concert: demand shifts out, there is excess
demand at the old price, surge pricing pulls in drivers (movement along supply) and deters lower-value riders (movement along
demand). Rides go to people who value them most.

**Elasticity.** Elasticity of demand is the percent change in quantity divided by the percent change in price, a unit-free
measure. If its absolute value is above 1 demand is elastic (quantity responds a lot); below 1 it is inelastic. A diabetic
buying insulin has nearly vertical demand; a commuter choosing between neighboring gas stations has nearly horizontal demand.
The more elastic the market, the more of a shock lands on quantity rather than price. Houston versus San Francisco: the same
demand growth raised prices in San Francisco (hard to build, inelastic supply) but raised quantity in Houston (easy to build).
Elasticity grows with time, so an oil spike such as the 2026 Strait of Hormuz disruption lands mostly on price in the short run
and gives most of it back in the long run.

**Four questions for comparative statics.** Which curve moved? Which way? Does the shock land on price or quantity (the
elasticity of the other curve)? Short run or long run? Solyndra is the trap of reading a short-run price spike as permanent:
polysilicon prices fell about 89% from roughly $460/kg to $52/kg by 2011 and its edge vanished. High prices invite the supply
that competes them away.

**Jevons paradox.** When steam engines got more fuel efficient, British coal use rose. Cheaper AI may likewise raise total
compute use. Resource use rises ("backfire") exactly when demand elasticity times the input's cost share is greater than 1.

**Reading shocks backwards.** Price and quantity moving together give four fingerprints: both up means demand shifted out;
price up with quantity down means supply shifted in; price down with quantity up means supply shifted out; both down means
demand shifted in. If both curves move, the data do not cleanly reveal either one.

**Learning about demand.** You never observe the demand curve directly. Ways to learn it: understand your value relative to
substitutes, market research, market data, and A/B tests that randomize price (ZipRecruiter assigned new customers to price
buckets from $19 to $399; Uber's rounding of surge multipliers acts as an "as if" experiment and one study estimated about $7
billion of UberX consumer surplus in 2015). Amazon doubled its fulfillment footprint during COVID, mistaking a surge for a
lasting shift, and spent years right-sizing.

**Takeaways.** Demand is marginal benefit; own-price changes are movements and everything else is a shift; elasticity sets
whether a shock lands on price or quantity; next lecture drops perfect competition for monopoly.

## Lecture 5: Introduction to Monopoly

**Price taker versus price maker.** A competitive price taker "sees a price": its perceived demand is perfectly elastic, so it
only chooses quantity and follows the golden rule MC(q) = P (a wheat farmer, an Uber driver, someone selling a share on the
NASDAQ). A price maker "sees a demand curve" and picks a point on it (Novo Nordisk with Ozempic, Taylor Swift tickets, the
only beer stand in a stadium). Monopoly is a model: it treats the demand curve as fixed, whereas in reality a price change can
provoke rivals' reactions that shift demand (oligopoly comes later). All businesses face competitive pressure.

**How much should a price maker sell? Rise & Grind with the station concession.** Prices for 1 to 8 batches are $34, $31,
$28, $25, $22, $19, $16, $13 and the marginal cost of those batches is $6, $8, $10, $12, $14, $16, $18, $20; the $40 oven
lease is sunk. The best plan is four batches at $25 for a profit of $24. The tempting answer of six batches (where price
equals marginal cost) earns only $8, so the price-taker golden rule is wrong for a price maker.

**Marginal revenue.** Demand here is Qd = 37 - p, i.e. inverse demand P(q) = 37 - 3q. Selling one more unit has three effects:
revenue from new customers (the new unit sells at P), lost revenue from the lower price on units already selling, and the
extra cost of producing the unit. Marginal revenue is MR = P(q) + P'(q)q. Going from 3 to 4 batches gives 25 minus $9 of price
cuts on the three existing batches, so MR(4) = $16. For linear demand, MR has the same intercept and is twice as steep, so
MR(q) = 37 - 6q. A price taker does not affect price, so MR = P and the rule collapses to P = MC. The lesson: the rule is
always MR = MC, then read the price off the demand curve. A monopolist's extra unit cuts the price on all units, so MR < P and it
produces less (Enzo Ferrari: always deliver one car less than the market demands). Worked example (County Fair): Qd = 20 - 0.5p,
MC = 8, so P(q) = 40 - 2q, MR = 40 - 4q, q = 8, price $24, variable profit (24 - 8) x 8 = $128.

**Implications.** The Sunday matinee with capacity 100 and MC of $10 per attendee earns nothing if it fills the house at $10;
MR = MC at 50 seats priced at $35, a $1,250 profit box. Fixed costs do not change price or quantity because they enter neither
MR nor MC; they only affect whether to operate at all ("I need to earn back the rent" is the fixed-cost trap). Only demand and
the cost curve bound a monopolist's profit. Value-based pricing means pricing off willingness to pay; its opposite is
cost-plus pricing, a rule-of-thumb markup. Parker-Hannifin moved from a 35% markup on everything to pricing off substitutability,
and Disney parks moved from one flat price to calendar-based prices in 2016.

**Two-part tariffs.** A single monopoly price leaves deadweight loss (value never created) and consumer surplus (value not
captured). With a two-part tariff (entry fee F plus per-unit price P), the optimal choice is P = MC and F = the consumer surplus
at that price. In the Disney example with MC = 0, P = 0 and F = $200 creates and captures all the value. Costco is a close
example: membership revenue (about $5 billion) is around 75% of profit; Amazon Prime has a similar structure. When it breaks
down is the next lecture.

**Bonus: markup rule and Lerner index.** With MR = P(1 + 1/e) (e being the elasticity of demand) the optimal price is
P = MC / (1 + 1/e): more elastic demand means a lower markup. For MC = 10, e = -2 gives P = 20 and e = -4 gives about 13.3; a price
taker (e = minus infinity) prices at MC. The Lerner index (P - MC)/P = -1/e measures market power, ranging from 0 to 1, and is
0 for a competitive firm. A monopolist never prices in the inelastic region of demand, so it does not score 1.

**Takeaways.** Monopoly power is the ability to move the market price; every firm sets MR = MC; price depends on marginal cost
and demand, not fixed cost; a two-part tariff captures more than any single price.

## Lecture 6: Price Discrimination, Part I: Segment (Group) Pricing

**Two-part tariffs with MC above zero.** Disney example recap: with MC = 0, charging per ride at the best single price ($5,
20 rides, $100) leaves $50 of rider surplus and $50 of value never created; rides at P = 0 with a $200 gate fee capture the
whole triangle. The optimal two-part tariff is P = MC and F = the consumer surplus at that price. Familiar examples are golf
clubs, utilities, phone and data plans, Costco and Amazon Prime. County Fair example (P(q) = 40 - 2q, MC = 8): the per-unit
price is $8, so 16 units sell; the fee is the surplus triangle, half of 16 times 32, which is $256. Usage revenue of $128
just covers the cost, so the profit is the $256 fee, against $128 from the best single price (the slide totals fee plus usage
revenue as $384). Why not everywhere? Arbitrage (a member could buy at MC and resell), uncertainty (if F is set too high you
lose the whole market, not a few sales) and heterogeneity (one fee is too high for some customers and too low for others).

**What price discrimination is.** A single price cannot capture surplus from high-WTP customers or create value for
low-WTP customers. Price discrimination means adding a different markup over cost to different buyers; price differences that
merely reflect cost differences do not count. Senior movie discounts, overnight shipping and business fares are all likely
price discrimination, in different mixes with cost differences. Third-degree price discrimination is segment (group) pricing:
each group faces one price but prices differ across groups. First-degree is individualized pricing, in effect third-degree with
groups of one and perfect insight into willingness to pay.

**Three conditions.** To price differently across groups a seller must (1) identify types, (2) separate them by actually charging
different prices, and (3) prevent arbitrage (resale from the low-price group to the high-price group). It is only worth doing
if buyers genuinely differ in willingness to pay. The Force: the optimal price depends on a customer's WTP and price
responsiveness, and less elastic segments tolerate higher markups.

**Examples.** Airlines split business from leisure travelers (when someone else pays, demand is less elastic) using signals
such as a weekend stay-over, last-minute booking or a Thursday return to O'Hare. Other examples include discounts for seniors and
students, financial aid, Hawaii's kama'aina rate, geography, negotiated car prices and app-mediated transactions. College tuition
discounts go to more desirable students and students with more options.

**ClipperJet worked example.** Marginal cost is $2 per seat. Business demand is P = 10 - Q and leisure demand is P = 6 - Q.
Separate prices: business MR = 10 - 2Q = 2 gives Q = 4, P = $6 and contribution $16; leisure gives Q = 2, P = $4 and contribution
$4, total $20. One price: market demand is the horizontal sum (above $6 only business buys; below it both do, Q = 16 - 2P, with a
kink at P = $6, Q = 4); below the kink P = 8 - 0.5Q, MR = 8 - Q = 2 gives Q = 6, P = $5, contribution $18. Discriminating is worth
$2 here. The more different the segments' ideal prices, the bigger the gain.

**When conditions break.** Arbitrage: buyers resell (someone using a senior discount for someone else). Identification: without
data on who has high WTP, group pricing loses value (ride-hailing apps go beyond traditional discrimination but face limits and
pushback). Separation: legal limits (race, religion, gender), customer backlash, product segmentation workarounds such as the
"Pink Tax," and shoppers disguising themselves to look like low-WTP buyers to airline search engines.

**Are big firms leaving money on the table?** ZipRecruiter randomly assigned new customers to ten price buckets from $19 to
$399 around a base of $99; demand at $99 was inelastic (estimated elasticity about -0.33) and the best single price was $327. In a
follow-up, an algorithmic per-buyer price using 133 characteristics beat the best single price by 19% in profit and the $99
status quo by 86%; discriminating does not mean charging everyone more. Retail chains price almost identically across stores in
different-income areas, and the median chain could earn about $16.1 million more per year (1.6% of revenue) with store-level
prices. Movie theaters discriminate on time and viewer type but essentially never on which film, and researchers found the usual
defenses unconvincing.

**Takeaways.** Two-part tariffs price at MC and take the surplus as a fee, limited by arbitrage, uncertainty and heterogeneity.
Charge a higher markup to the less elastic segment. Identify, separate and prevent arbitrage. The trap is one price for
segments that differ. Menu pricing (versioning) comes next.

## Lecture 7: Price Discrimination, Part II: Menu Pricing

**Second-degree price discrimination (menu pricing).** The seller offers every buyer the same menu of options and buyers
self-select. Unlike third-degree pricing it does not require identifying or separating types, and sometimes needs no arbitrage
protection. The three varieties are versioning (options vary by quality), bundling (options vary by composition) and quantity
discounts (options vary by quantity). It is imperfect: to make the high type pick the premium option the seller must leave
them some surplus, an information rent, so some value is not created or not captured.

**Versioning.** Profitable when consumers differ in how much they value quality; resale is not a concern and there is no need
to identify types. Coffee example: the regular person values nice coffee at $2.00 and the snob at $2.50, marginal cost is $1.00.
Adding a super coffee (regular WTP $2.80, snob WTP $5.00, marginal cost $1.40) lets buyers sort themselves. The recipe for two
types and two preset qualities: (0) confirm the high type values the upgrade more than the low type, (1) price the low-quality
product at the low type's willingness to pay, (2) price the high-quality product at the low price plus the high type's WTP for
the upgrade (so the high type is just indifferent), and (3) check whether selling only one version does better. In the coffee
case Nice is priced at $2 and Super at $4.50, for a profit of $4.10, beating selling only Super to the snob ($3.60), Super to
everyone ($2.80) or Nice to everyone ($2.00). The common wrong answer is a Super price of $5. Airline analogy: economy priced
to regular travelers, business priced at economy plus the business traveler's WTP for the upgrade (about $904 versus $254, a $650
upgrade), and business travelers keep some surplus. If both types value the upgrade equally, self-selection fails and the seller
offers just one product. Apple's expensive memory upgrades and freemium models (ChatGPT's Free, Plus and Pro tiers) are
versioning.

**Hobbling.** Deliberately damaging a product to separate types: lowering economy legroom hurts the business traveler's WTP
more than the leisure traveler's, so business travelers are less tempted to trade down. Examples: Intel's 80386 as a 80486 with the
floating-point unit switched off, third-class French trains with no roof, Mercedes charging $60 a month to unlock a faster EV,
and Tesla capping the battery of a car sold as the Model S 60D. It only pays if it lowers the high type's temptation to defect by
more than it costs.

**Bundling.** Selling goods that could be consumed separately as a package (cable TV, Microsoft Office, season tickets, albums).
Pure bundling sells only bundles; mixed bundling also sells items separately at a higher total price. Comcast example with
marginal cost 0: Gen X values Internet at $50 and cable TV at $110; Millennials value Internet at $120 and cable TV at $30. Sold
separately the best prices are $120 and $110, giving $230. As a bundle, Gen X values it at $160 and Millennials at $150, so a
$150 bundle sells to both for $300. Bundling works when willingness to pay is negatively correlated across types (no type is
high for everything), because bundling averages out WTP; it is especially profitable when marginal cost is low. It fails for
perfect substitutes. Solving bundling problems means checking every case (bundle only, bundle plus one item, bundle plus both
items). Everyday bundles include Spotify, Amazon Prime, Microsoft 365, Disney+/Hulu/ESPN+, Apple One and AI subscriptions;
Netflix attracts many types of viewers by offering many categories of content, while Marvel selling movies one at a time is not
a bundle.

**Quantity discounts.** Sometimes cost per unit differs, but often a discount is second-degree discrimination. Alice values a
first chocolate bar at $5 and a second at $3 (marginal cost $1); a two-pack at $8 captures all her surplus. With two types
(Alice $5 then $3, Bob $4.50 then $1.50), one price of $4.50 earns $7, while selling a single bar at $4.50 and a discounted
two-pack at $7.50 earns $9 (Alice takes the pack, Bob the single). The recipe is the same as versioning with quantity in the role
of quality.

**Force-Trap-Edge.** Menus sort buyers by their own choices at the price of an information rent. The trap is a menu that fails
to sort because the cheap option attracts everyone. The edge is versioning, bundling and hobbling.

## Lecture 8: The Long Run and Lessons in Competition

**Menu pricing recap example.** A bike originally priced at $2,245 was cut to $1,895 and joined by an upgraded Bike+ at $2,495:
versioning, where the price drop adds new customers (removing deadweight loss) and the added-feature bike stops the firm losing
too much surplus from high-value buyers.

**Brand X: value-based pricing.** Brand X priced by cost plus, which makes no sense for a price maker; it should set MR = MC,
needing a demand curve and marginal cost. The accounting unit cost of $2.55 per gallon (total costs of about $10.8 million over
4.26 million gallons; they priced at $3.06) mixes marginal and non-marginal costs. Only raw materials ($1.69) and hourly labor
($0.06) are caused by one more gallon, so MC = $1.75. Because Brand X nudged its price from quarter to quarter as input costs
moved, it accidentally ran A/B tests and the data trace out demand. The fitted line is Q = 4.98 - 0.245P, inverse demand
P = 20.3 - 4.08Q, and MR = 20.3 - 8.16Q. Setting MR = MC = $1.75 gives q = 2.28 million gallons and P = $11.02, far out of sample,
so raise price gradually, experiment and learn the curve.

**Perfect competition in the long run.** The assumptions are (1) an identical product (the law of one price; a commodity
requires exchanges and price transparency), (2) many small buyers and sellers (with few sellers each acts strategically, which is
oligopoly, Lectures 10 to 12), (3) everyone is a price taker (a consequence of 1 and 2), and (4) free entry and exit. The long
run is the time over which all relevant choices can be made. If price is above minimum average cost, firms make money and
others enter, pushing price down; if below, firms lose money and some exit, pushing price up. Entry and exit stop only at the
price where each firm sits at the bottom of its average cost curve, MC(q) = AC(q). If all firms have identical costs, long-run
supply is flat at that price and every seller earns zero economic profit ("zero" includes the opportunity cost of capital). The rule
for thinking like a microeconomist: no free lunch; if a deal looks great, ask why it is still available. Adam Smith's invisible
hand becomes a "dead hand" strangling profits.

**Monopolistic competition.** Differentiated products break the law of one price, so a firm faces its own downward-sloping demand
curve and sets MR = MC, but with free entry other sellers offer their own versions, each entrant takes some of your customers and
shifts your demand in until demand is tangent to average cost. You still set your own price and still make zero profit.
Restaurants, dry cleaners, hotels, wineries and food trucks are examples. Price-making power is not sufficient for
profitability; what is sufficient is restricted entry (licenses, patents, the only site in town) or an advantage rivals cannot
copy (geology under your oil field). LaCroix's flavors and Spotify are discussion cases.

**Sources of durable advantage.** Any edge that rivals can copy gets competed away. Durable ones include natural endowments
(geology, location), economies of scale or scope at a size rivals cannot reach, compounding learning or process advantages,
favorable rules or subsidies (both major airplane makers get subsidies and sue each other at the WTO) and a genuinely different
business model. Costco pays more than competitors but keeps lower unit cost because turnover and shrinkage fall by more than
wages rise, though nothing stops a rival from raising wages. FedEx's hub-and-spoke model and Amazon's scale are asked about with
the test "does size actually drive unit costs down?" Cost advantages are usually hard to maintain (think Moneyball).

**Takeaways.** With free entry, long-run economic profit goes to zero in both perfect and monopolistic competition; price-making
alone is not enough. The escape is a durable, hard-to-mimic advantage or genuinely restricted entry. Next: oligopoly, after
uncertainty and game theory. A bonus segue asks whether you can get rich driving for Uber (tipping and equilibrium wages).

## Lecture 9: The Value of Information

**Decision trees.** A tree has decision nodes (squares, where you choose), chance nodes (circles, where the world chooses with
probabilities) and payoffs at the end of every path. Draw forward and solve backward: take the expected value at each circle and
the best branch at each square. Grocery example: going to the store (salmon worth $15 if fresh, $5 if not; chicken $12 always;
fresh half the time) gives 0.5 x 15 + 0.5 x 12 = $13.50, beating going home (best option $13 from an Italian dinner that pays
$16 or $10 depending on the kids). The trap is "commit, then look": choosing the protein before seeing the fish would make the store
look worth only $12. Order matters: you see the fish first, so the circle comes before the square, and the $1.50 difference is
what it is worth to know before choosing. Rule for thinking like a microeconomist: use decision trees to name your assumptions,
then think backwards.

**Merck case (a vaccine decision under uncertainty).** In September 2020, Dr. Okuna can launch vaccine version A now (worth $2.85
per dose in expected value) or wait three months for version B, which may fail or succeed with high or mediocre efficacy. If B
succeeds but is mediocre it can be sold for about $2 minus a reputation cost R, or licensed for $1. R is unknown, so put it in the
tree: waiting is worth between $2.97 and $3.27 whatever R turns out to be, which beats $2.85, so she waits. This is sensitivity
analysis: solve across the range of the unknown, look for a pivot point where the choice would flip, and note that a missing number
is not the end of the analysis. Assuming launching A stays possible ("the door swings both ways") moves the margin but not the
choice. The course uses expected value (risk neutrality) because risks are small or diversifiable; risk aversion is handled with
expected utility and covered in finance.

**Value of information.** Information about a chance node is drawn by moving that node in front of the decision it informs;
its value is the expected value of the tree with the node moved minus the tree without. With R = 0.5 the tree folds to $3.12. A
sealed envelope revealing whether B succeeds is worth $0, because she waits either way (not pivotal). An envelope revealing how
effective B would be is worth $0.33 (0.4 x 4.35 + 0.6 x 2.85 = $3.45 versus $3.12), because if efficacy is mediocre she stops waiting
and launches A. Reordering a tree cannot create value; only acting on what you learn can. Principles: valuable information is
pivotal, it is worth most when you are close to indifferent and when you know least. Before paying for information ask "what would
I do differently once I had it?"

**Paying for information, good and bad.** Firms buy information directly (A/B tests, consultants, surveys, market data) and
indirectly by waiting and experimenting. Surprisingly valuable examples: Domino's taste tests that led to adding 40% more cheese,
and Procter & Gamble's Febreze, a 1996 failure as an odor-neutralizing spray until research revealed "noseblind" customers and a
"reward" role, leading to a general "last step spray" and a billion-dollar brand. Bad information collection includes over-
researching a $20 item, consulting reports that are rarely used ("information as corporate furniture") and unnecessary medical tests
(one study estimated that 90% of physicians regularly order unnecessary procedures at about $46 billion per year in the US, much of
it defensive medicine).

**Bonus: option value and experimentation.** Diablo Canyon example: the plant loses $100m this year; next year the price rises
permanently (plant makes $100m a year) or falls permanently (loses $200m a year) with equal chance, at a 10% discount rate. The
simple tree of operating forever gives an expected value of -$600m, so shut down. But the option to wait a year and then decide
gives 0.5 x 900 + 0.5 x (-100) = $400m against $0 for shutting down now; that is option value. Option value exists when waiting
is possible, valuable (pivotal) information will arrive, and the action is at least partly irreversible (a one-way door).
Reversibility is also what lets you experiment: Jeep switching a sunroof supplier (a 75% chance of a bad outcome worth -1 and 25%
of a good outcome worth 2) has expected value -0.25 if stuck with the choice, but 0.21 if it can switch back next year, so a
worse bet today can be worth taking in order to learn.

**Takeaways.** Draw forward, solve backward; name missing numbers and solve anyway; information is worth something only if it is
pivotal; next comes game theory, a multi-player decision tree solved by backward induction.
