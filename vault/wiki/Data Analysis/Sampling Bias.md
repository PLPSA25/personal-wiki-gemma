# Sampling Bias

Sampling bias occurs when the method used to select a sample systematically favors certain outcomes over others, leading to a non-representative sample of the target population. This bias can arise from issues with the sampling frame, the selection process, or nonresponse. To mitigate this, it is important to use methods like simple random sampling and to understand how the sample was selected.

## Key points

- Sampling frame bias: the list you draw from does not match the target population. ([[raw/Data and Decisions Lectures|Data and Decisions Lectures, Lecture 3]])
- Self-selection or voluntary response bias: samples of volunteers, such as Twitter polls (bias comes from who visits the site and from the link between answering and being positively disposed). ([[raw/Data and Decisions Lectures|Data and Decisions Lectures, Lecture 3]])
- Survivorship bias: analysis based only on the subset that clears a hurdle. ([[raw/Data and Decisions Lectures|Data and Decisions Lectures, Lecture 3]])
- Convenience sampling bias: individuals easily accessible to the surveyor; social networks are not random (homophily). ([[raw/Data and Decisions Lectures|Data and Decisions Lectures, Lecture 3]])
- Measurement bias: measured value = true value + systematic error (bias) + random error (imprecision). ([[raw/Data and Decisions Lectures|Data and Decisions Lectures, Lecture 3]])
- The best way to get a representative sample is a simple random sample (SRS): by design any characteristic in the population, including unobservables, is uncorrelated with being included, so on average the sample mimics its sampling frame. ([[raw/Data and Decisions Lectures|Data and Decisions Lectures, Lecture 3]])
