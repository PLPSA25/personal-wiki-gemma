# Menu Pricing

Menu pricing involves offering buyers a single menu of options, allowing them to self-select based on their preferences. This type of price discrimination can take forms such as versioning, bundling, or quantity discounts. The profitability often depends on creating an information rent by allowing some customers to receive a better deal than the seller ideally desires.

## Key points

- Second-degree price discrimination (menu pricing) does not require identifying or separating types, and sometimes needs no arbitrage protection. ([[raw/Economics Lectures|Economics Lectures, Lecture 7]])
- The three varieties are versioning (options vary by quality), bundling (options vary by composition), and quantity discounts (options vary by quantity). ([[raw/Economics Lectures|Economics Lectures, Lecture 7]])
- To make the high type pick the premium option, the seller must leave them some surplus, an information rent, so some value is not created or not captured. ([[raw/Economics Lectures|Economics Lectures, Lecture 7]])
- Quantity discounts can be profitable because customers differ in how much they value additional units. ([[raw/Menu Pricing.docx|Menu Pricing]])
- Bundling works when willingness to pay is negatively correlated across types, because bundling averages out WTP; it is especially profitable when marginal cost is low. ([[raw/Economics Lectures|Economics Lectures, Lecture 7]])
- The seller has to leave that customer some surplus because, if the large pack were priced too aggressively, the frequent player could instead choose the smaller package intended for low-demand customers. ([[raw/Menu Pricing.docx|Menu Pricing]])
