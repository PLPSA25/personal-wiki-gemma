# Durable Advantage

A durable advantage is an edge that rivals cannot easily copy, which allows a business to maintain profitability in a market where free entry would otherwise drive profits to zero. This protection often stems from factors like unique assets, scale, or customer lock-in, rather than just product differentiation. The strength of this advantage can be eroded by changes in the environment or rival innovation.

## Key points

- Durable ones include natural endowments (geology, location), economies of scale or scope at a size rivals cannot reach, compounding learning or process advantages, favorable rules or subsidies (both major airplane makers get subsidies and sue each other at the WTO) and a genuinely different business model. ([[raw/Economics Lectures|Economics Lectures, Lecture 8]])
- With free entry, long-run economic profit goes to zero in both perfect and monopolistic competition; price-making alone is not enough. ([[raw/Economics Lectures|Economics Lectures, Lecture 8]])
- The escape is a durable, hard-to-mimic advantage or genuinely restricted entry. ([[raw/Economics Lectures|Economics Lectures, Lecture 8]])
- What protects Apple is more than product differentiation. Its ecosystem makes the iPhone more valuable when used with other Apple’s products (e.g., Mac, Apple Watch, AirPods, iPad or iCloud) or services (e.g., App Store). ([[raw/Durable Advantage.docx|Durable Advantage]])
- This protection is strengthened as each additional device or service ties customers more closely to Apple. ([[raw/Durable Advantage.docx|Durable Advantage]])
- It would weaken if regulation forced greater interoperability, Apple damaged its reputation (e.g., poor quality or decreased innovation), or a rival built an innovative and equally trusted ecosystem that made switching simple and inexpensive. ([[raw/Durable Advantage.docx|Durable Advantage]])
