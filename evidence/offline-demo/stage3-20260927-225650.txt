    **Economic versus accounting cost.** An accountant measures all expenditures and allocates them across activities, which is
    useful for understanding what has happened. Economic cost is forward looking and depends on the value of alternatives:

    **Opportunity cost.** The opportunity cost of an action is the value of the most highly valued alternative given up. Making

    Opportunity cost of time is a relevant economic cost. Nothing you own is free: the Tokyo firm's basement farm costs its
    expenditure plus the profit forgone from the next best use of that real estate.

    **Inventory.** An item's economic cost is its replacement cost if you will need another unit, or its resale value if not;
    the price you paid is sunk. A bike shop that bought at $750 while the price rose to $1,000 should treat $1,000 as its cost.
    The same logic applies to businesses stocking up ahead of tariffs: if they will restock at the tariffed price, they should
    raise prices now.

    **Cost disease (Baumol).** When productivity in a sector does not keep pace with rising wages elsewhere, its costs climb.
    Playing a Beethoven quartet takes the same 2.66 hours as in 1826, but the opportunity cost of that labor is about 23 times
    higher (real wages from $1.14 to $26.44). Higher education, health care, the performing arts and personal services are
    affected; fast technology growth and the ability to shift inputs protect against it.

    **Sunk costs and institutions.** Kodak invented much of digital photography yet could not pivot after dominating film.

    **Why microeconomics.** Smart people fall into predictable traps: "we already put $2 billion in, we can't walk away" (sunk
